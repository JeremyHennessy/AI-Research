# Pass 47 — Genome boot-up compatibility is not the same as chromosome swapping or de novo cellular origin

**Source-critical, research-only review | 10 October 2026 | two E2 original full-main studies; one E1 original abstract comparator.** This is a distinct causal review following [Pass 46 on genome-inactivated recipient rescue](139-pass46-revival-of-genomically-inactivated-cells.md) and [Pass 45 on minimal-cell founding](138-pass45-minimal-cell-lineage-vs-de-novo-construction.md). Neither paper is repeated or upgraded here. No wet-lab or computational experiments, organisms, simulations, models, source-code execution, training, costs, production change, new automation or other repository modification was undertaken.

## 1. Question, source search and bounded finding

**Question:** How much evidence exists for genome-directed cellular replication *outside one narrow transplant-compatible chassis*, and how much does successful chromosome engineering in an independently developed bacterial platform establish about transplanting a whole genome into a nonreplicating founder?

**Original-source search outcome:** No **independent 2026 founder-resolved replication of Seidel's genomic-inactivation-and-revival assay** was verified in this bounded search. Search included the original 2026 preprint and its cited earlier transplant literature, primary 2016 Mollicutes cross-species study, 2018 `Mesoplasma florum` transplant follow-up, and a 2021 independently developed `Escherichia coli` chromosome exchange program. This is a **documented evidence gap**, not a systematic review proving that no other paper exists. The gap is not cured by relabeling a different genomic transfer method as independent replication.

**What the sources genuinely add:** The Bordeaux/Sherbrooke-led 2016 paper extends successful whole-genome transplantation to **multiple Mollicutes**, including one crossing to the `Mesoplasma` genus. Yet `Spiroplasma` donors failed and a closely related `M. mycoides` subspecies transplanted poorly. Its plasmid `oriC` compatibility assays were positive even for unsuccessful *whole-genome* transfers, so replication-origin recognition alone cannot explain boot-up. The separate Rikkyo University 2021 study built viable **tripartite-chromosome `E. coli`**, transferred some isolated megabase-sized chromosomes, and swapped chromosome segments between **already living, engineered E. coli**. This is substantial genome manipulation outside mycoplasmas, but *not full genome replacement of a genome-inactivated E. coli cell* and not constructing a cell from nonliving components.

**Causal distinction:** All experiments began with viable, already organized cells or cellular machinery. Successful daughter growth shows living-cell genetic continuity once a compatible system is operating. Whether an entirely nonreplicating *founder* was rescued must be established separately from marker selection, phenotype, chromosome identity, or the viability of a recipient cell before engineering.

## 2. Labroussaa et al. 2016 — transplantation has measurable compatibility and source-preparation barriers

**Original full paper:** Fabien Labroussaa, Anne Lebaudy, Vincent Baby et al., *Impact of donor–recipient phylogenetic distance on bacterial genome transplantation*, `Nucleic Acids Research` 44(17):8501–8511; published online **3 August 2016**, DOI [10.1093/nar/gkw688](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkw688), [Oxford full Results, Methods and Discussion](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/44/17/8501/2468051), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27488189/). **Evidence grade E2 original publisher full main** (not E3 independent reproduction or supplementary/raw sequencing reanalysis). **Rights:** original **CC BY-NC 4.0**, with any separately credited material treated separately. This review reproduces no original figure, table, genome sequence or substantial manuscript passage.

### Design and experimental units

Seven genomes of Spiroplasma-group Mollicutes were compared against a **single restriction-deficient `M. capricolum` recipient strain**, `McapΔRE`. Whole genomes were prepared from native bacterial cells or from engineered intermediary yeast hosts; recipient cells were living, genetically modified and supplied laboratory media. `tetM` antibiotic-resistance markers were engineered into donor genome sources to recover candidate transplant colonies. **“Selection-free” applies to Pass 46's separate protocol, not this 2016 method.**

For each species, the authors report **three independent transplantation experiments**, with transplantation-rate estimates calculated from an average of four GT events within each experiment. This is not twelve independent recipient species, twelve de novo founders or an experiment on genome-inactivated recipients. Ten putative transplants per species were checked by species-specific PCR, two by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis and the same two by whole-genome sequencing, with donor-type colonies and little/no detectable recipient chromosome material. These checks support genotype replacement **among sampled colonies**, but do not resolve the absolute probability that any single initially dead cell was revived.

### Positive results and the crucial failures

- **Six of seven donor choices**, counting self-background `M. capricolum`, yielded at least some **from-bacteria** transplant colonies. Five nonrecipient donor species succeeded at differing rates; `Spiroplasma citri` did **not** yield successful whole-genome transplants despite its `oriC` plasmid replicating in the recipient.
- In the original Table 1's **from-bacteria** comparison, transplants per microgram of genome DNA (mean ± **SEM**) were roughly **238 ± 39** for same-species `M. capricolum`, **196 ± 89** for `M. leachii`, **74.4 ± 9.66** for `M. mycoides` subsp. capri, **18.8 ± 1.27** for `M. putrefaciens`, **3.43 ± 2.86** for `M. mycoides` subsp. mycoides and **3.02 ± 5.23** for `M. florum`. `S. citri` was **zero**. These are conditioned on chosen inputs and a specific recipient, *not independent new-origin frequencies or universal viability thresholds*.
- The most distant successful donor was **`Mesoplasma florum`**, approximately **92.47% core-proteome similarity** under this study's 79-protein comparison. This was a cross-**genus** `Mycoplasma`-recipient transplantation, not a new bacterial phylum or cell built from inorganic ingredients.
- **From-yeast** transplant assays succeeded for `M. leachii`, `M. mycoides` subsp. capri, `M. putrefaciens`, and `M. florum`; `M. mycoides` subsp. mycoides and `S. citri` gave **zero**. Direct versus yeast-donor outcomes differed, including for the closely related `M. mycoides` subspecies. An independent mechanistic inference cannot be made from phylogenetic rank alone.
- An additional `Spiroplasma floricola` attempt was **unsuccessful**, but marked as **“data not shown”** in the original. Treat this as author-reported negative, with no audited denominator, rather than a quantitatively demonstrated second negative cohort.

A donor genome concentration around **2 micrograms** gave the highest transplant yields in the comparative curves; more DNA could *reduce* yield. The literature does not establish why: impurities, physical delivery constraints, recipient stress and enzymatic compatibility can interact. The `S. citri` donor genome was the **largest** and among the **slowest growing** in the study, confounding simple attribution of failure to genetic distance. Conversely, related `M. mycoides` subsp. mycoides also transferred unexpectedly poorly, showing that distance is not a complete model.

### Negative mechanistic control that matters for synthetic-life research

All tested `oriC`-bearing donor plasmids could replicate within the restriction-deficient recipient, even those whose **whole chromosome could not be transplanted successfully**. The authors infer that functions beyond triggering DNA replication—compatibility of transcription, translation, other non-genomic functions, genome size, mobile elements or physical entry—can limit boot-up. The data support this *constraint*, not a direct experimental proof of exactly which missing protein caused a specific failed transplant.

**Independence limit:** The 2016 author affiliations include Bordeaux/INRA, Université de Sherbrooke, and one JCVI collaborator (Sanjay Vashee). The design expands species and cross-genus range with substantial non-JCVI participants, but it still uses the inherited `M. capricolum` transplantation chassis and does not independently repeat the 2026 chemical-genome inactivation/revival endpoint. Restriction-deficient recipients, prepared genome carriers, marker selection and yeast-mediated assembly are researcher-controlled dependencies.

## 3. Baby et al. 2018 — dependent source reveals mutation after transplant, not new independent origin

**Original primary bibliographic and abstract:** Vincent Baby, Fabien Labroussaa, Joëlle Brodeur et al., *Cloning and Transplantation of the Mesoplasma florum Genome*, `ACS Synthetic Biology` 7(1):209–217, first online **3 October 2017**, issue date **19 January 2018**, DOI [10.1021/acssynbio.7b00279](https://doi.org/10.1021/acssynbio.7b00279), [original publisher summary and rights](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acssynbio.7b00279), [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28893065/).

**Grade E1 abstract/publisher metadata only:** The publisher's main article is purchase-restricted in this review, and the full final Results/Methods, variant-table denominators and supplements were **not inspected**. Do not upgrade the study to E2, assert a specific number of mutations per transplant, or treat author statements as independent raw-sequence reanalysis. **Rights:** publisher shows **© 2017 American Chemical Society**; no reuse permission for protected body/figures established. No source text, figures or data are copied.

**Reported findings:** The Bordeaux/Sherbrooke team cloned `M. florum` genomic DNA in `Saccharomyces cerevisiae` and transplanted it back to living `M. capricolum` recipient cells. The original abstract reports whole-genome sequencing of transplants revealed **some new genetic variants** in every evaluated `M. florum` transplant; even same-species `M. capricolum`→`M. capricolum` transfers generated mutations at a lower frequency. Three successive researcher-directed transplant rounds showed **no detectable efficiency change**, contrary to a simple hypothesis that transplant-induced mutations inevitably accumulate a strong selectable boot-up improvement. This is a source-reported finding, with unknown exact variant counts/effect sizes from full-paper access.

**Why not pooled replication:** Labroussaa and Baby coauthored the 2016 study and this is the same Bordeaux/Sherbrooke experimental network. This 2018 paper is a useful **dependent follow-up**, not independent confirmation of de novo cell construction, and not three unassisted autonomous generations merely because the protocol was repeated three times. A mutation detected after a transplant is not automatically an adaptive innovation; neutral sequence changes, stress, source-genome heterogeneity and selection among survivors are alternatives.

## 4. Yoneji et al. 2021 — an independent E. coli chromosome-replacement strategy, different measured endpoint

**Original full paper:** Tatsuya Yoneji, Hironobu Fujita, Takahito Mukai and Masayuki Su'etsugu, *Grand scale genome manipulation via chromosome swapping in Escherichia coli programmed by three one megabase chromosomes*, `Nucleic Acids Research` 49(15):8407–8418, published **28 April 2021**, DOI [10.1093/nar/gkab298](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkab298), [Oxford original full main text](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/49/15/8407/6255700), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8421210/). **E2 full main original** (not E3, supplementary protocols, raw PFGE imaging or genomes reprocessed). **Rights:** CC BY **4.0**, distinct from 2016 CC BY-NC. The authors are at Rikkyo University in Japan, a **separate research group**, yet investigated a **different scientific endpoint**, not an independent chemical-revival replication. The original declares co-founder/equity/CSO ties for Masayuki Su'etsugu at OriCiro Genomics, Inc. around related chromosome-replication technologies.

**What they actually achieved:** A previously viable, genome-reduced ~**3-Mb `E. coli`** was genetically partitioned into **three approximately 1-Mb circular chromosomes**, each paired to viable replication and segregation machinery. Some of these chromosomes were independently purified and introduced into **living competent bacteria**; other exchanges used conjugal transfer between already engineered strains. In two three-chromosome strain configurations, PFGE evidence suggested retention of the multipartite arrangement after **more than 100 laboratory propagation generations without antibiotic selection**. This is positive biological genetic inheritance, but a lab-maintained cell-lineage **is not** a new abiotic founder and removing antibiotics is not equivalent to withdrawing all researcher-provided culture and genetic-scaffold support.

**Specific positive and disconfirming observations:** Their `RGF138` and `RGF140` strains grew about **2× and 1.5× more slowly**, respectively, than the parental strain under the stated growth curve assay. One configuration (`RGF138`) maintained relatively typical rods with microscopy live-cell viability **78%** versus parent **80%**, while `RGF140` often became filamentous with only **53%** scored live in the same assay. These are cell-level microscopy percentages, **not the number of independent founding E. coli lineages**. Arrangement and replication timing can impair function even when all gene content is present.

- A purified ~**0.84-Mb `ChrLR`** was introduced into competent cells, but one reporter experiment obtained **two validated colonies from four commercial recipient-cell vials**. Do not equate that readout with population-wide high-efficiency genome bootstrapping.
- For the ~**1.02-Mb `ChrTer`**, **6 of 110 antibiotic-resistant screened colonies** carried an intact chromosome by PCR/PFGE. Selection for an antibiotic marker by itself is not equivalent to confirmation of whole-chromosome transfer.
- The native-origin ~**1.12-Mb `ChrOri`** did **not** yield electroporated colonies in the tested recipient cells, even though the chromosome could be exchanged via **conjugation** between suitably engineered genomes. Proposed interference from duplicating a replication origin or growth burden is a plausible interpretation, not a uniquely proven molecular cause.
- The study reports failure to transfer megabase chromosomes into its **own prepared DGF-298-derived competent cell preparation** even though commercial cells could accept some; strain context and competence-preparation effects remain possible confounders.

**Boundary and counterhypothesis:** Chromosome “swapping” here means a selective exchange of a **piece** of a partitioned chromosome set within live `E. coli` species, retaining most other preexisting information and viable cytoplasm. It does **not** produce a cell whose original entire chromosome has been made nonfunctional and then replaced at the one-cell founder level. Stable chromosome partitioning is not analogous to self-assembling the initial cellular membrane, making all translation factors from basic nutrients, or independent adaptation to an unforeseeable ecological setting.

## 5. Cross-study causal table — why headline successes cannot be added together

| Distinct evidence gate | 2016 Bordeaux/Sherbrooke/JCVI | 2018 dependent follow-up | 2021 independent Rikkyo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Living initial recipient? | **Yes**, restriction-deficient `M. capricolum` | **Yes**, `M. capricolum` | **Yes**, engineered `E. coli` |
| What genome-scale change? | Whole donor Mollicutes genomes into one recipient chassis | Same whole-genome family, with mutation after transfer | Split, electroporated and selectively swapped ~1-Mb chromosomes within viable `E. coli` |
| Genome recipients originally nonviable? | **Not tested** | **Not tested** | **Not tested** |
| Independent systems/programs? | Mixed Bordeaux/Sherbrooke + JCVI collaborator; one experimental platform | Overlapping authors/same program as 2016 | Separate Japan laboratory, but **different endpoint** |
| Explicit failures? | `S. citri` 0; `S. floricola` 0 reported without data; poor `Mmm`; full genome failure despite `oriC` plasmid replication | Author-reported acquired mutations, absent enrichment over three assisted rounds | `ChrOri` electroporation 0; some cells sick/filamentous; viability drop to 53%; transfer-dependent recipient context |
| Founding from raw nonliving ingredients? | **No** | **No** | **No** |
| Unassisted adaptive ecological reproduction? | **No** | **No** | **No** |

**Top scientific inference:** A genetic sequence may **encode instructions** but cannot be assumed to bootstrap in *any* cellular context. A working replication origin is necessary for some specific processes but not a sufficient readout for complete genome startup. Likewise, stable heredity of an engineered multipartite genome is real but does not automatically validate a bottom-up cellular origin or a new useful ecological function.

## 6. Falsifiable future-only hypotheses (none implemented or tested)

The following are *future evidence discrimination questions* and **not experimental protocols, production instructions or an authorization to construct organisms**:

1. **P47-H1 — Full-genome compatiblity is more restrictive than `oriC`-plasmid replication.** Replication of a small origin-bearing plasmid will be a weak predictor of successful complete chromosome takeover across divergent donors. **Counterexample:** independent datasets show matching presence/absence and rank of whole-chromosome establishment using `oriC` plasmid readouts once dose/strain controls are matched.
2. **P47-H2 — Phylogenetic distance is not an exclusive causal variable.** Recipient bootstrap compatibility will be influenced by donor source, genome physical integrity, epigenetic protection and species-specific functional machinery as well as evolutionary similarity. **Counterexample:** well-powered within-matched distance cohorts consistently show no residual dose/source/genome-size or enzymatic interaction.
3. **P47-H3 — Donor source can change near-zero outcomes.** Whole genomic DNA prepared in different living intermediates may change founding efficiency even with the same DNA sequence. **Counterexample:** complete equivalence under independent matched identity, molecule-integrity and DNA input checks.
4. **P47-H4 — A survivor-selected colony is not automatically a correctly booted full genome.** Antibiotic-positive survivors may sometimes contain marker-only recombinants or partial genetic material; orthogonal chromosome-wide identity matters. **Counterexample:** comprehensive, prospectively registered donor-identity data demonstrate no marker-only positives across recipient genotypes with homologous recombination.
5. **P47-H5 — Genome transfer can induce genetic damage without productive novel function.** Transplant-associated variants need not confer an adapted new cellular capability or enhance subsequent transfer success. **Counterexample:** independently verified new variants with causal increase of long-term descendants and external functional performance after removal of direct selection.
6. **P47-H6 — Stable multipartite genetic inheritance can carry fitness costs.** Some stable chromosome partitions will show reproducible growth or segregation penalties even while maintaining inherited DNA over many transfers. **Counterexample:** multiple independently derived, context-shifted partition designs with uniformly nondegrading morphology, fitness and loss rates.
7. **P47-H7 — A partial chromosome swap cannot validate complete dead-cell revival.** A viable recipient inheriting two native chromosomes can accept a third independently of any capacity to restart a cell whose original genome is wholly nonfunctional. **Counterexample:** proven direct causal equivalence in identical founders with all preexisting genome functions eliminated and no live genotype carryover.
8. **P47-H8 — Three papers do not count as three independent origination events.** Papers sharing lab ancestry, the same recipient chassis or an engineered precursor only furnish conditional validations of distinct links, not independent replication of abiotic cellular founding. **Counterexample:** multiple independent labs with verified nonviable or unorganized founders, full-genome provenance, robust daughter function and no active specialist machinery supplied during bootstrap.

**Future evaluation fields:** preintervention recipient viability and chromosome complement; donor full-length identity, repair and sequence provenance; valid denominators (donor molecules, recipient cells, screened/confirmed colonies); independent recipient, lab and founder counts; negative and rescued founders; measured chromosome inheritance in daughters; separation of ordinary food/energy from prepared essential machinery; relevant genetic, ecological and functional fitness outcome. **No such experiment was conducted or authorized here.**

**Still unverified:** A newer, genuinely independent, founder-resolved direct test that one chemically genome-inactivated recipient (not a rare viable survivor) yielded donor descendants without reusing an already active biological cytoplasm; whole-genome substitution into a *different* bacterial class is also not established by these sources. A lack of retrieved proof is **not evidence of impossibility**.
