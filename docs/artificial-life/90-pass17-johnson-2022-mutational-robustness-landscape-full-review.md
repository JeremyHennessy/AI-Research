# Pass 17 — Johnson & Desai (2022): evolution changes the accessibility of future mutational effects

**2026-10-08 | E2 full original eLife main text and methodology/figure results reviewed; no independent reproduction.** Milo S. Johnson & Michael M. Desai, [Mutational robustness changes during long-term adaptation in laboratory budding yeast populations](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.76491), *eLife* 11:e76491 (2022), [original journal article](https://elifesciences.org/articles/76491), [PMC original](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9355567/) (CC BY). Original barcode mutation fitness datasets, code/figures and supplementary ZIP were not independently downloaded or reanalyzed.

## Scientific question

When a yeast population gradually adapts in a steady environment, does the impact of **future possible mutations** remain unchanged, or do earlier fixed mutations alter its local landscape of genetic opportunities?

This is a specific and measurable change in a mature genotype–phenotype map, **not** a demonstration that a new organism, substrate rule, or memory system is invented.

## Design and proper statistical units

- Source material came from an **earlier long-term laboratory yeast evolution experiment** in **two constant environments**, carried forward for roughly **8,000–10,000 generations**. Study samples from that same history were used to interrogate changing effects of new disruptive mutations. This is additional causal/future-fitness measurement on reused evolution histories, **not another independently evolved 10,000 generations**.
- Authors sampled **six evolved populations in each environment**, **two clones per population** at **six timepoints**: **144 sampled clones**. These clones share long-term ancestors and are **not 144 independent evolution populations**.
- A barcode-insertion mutagenesis system evaluated fitness effects of **91 specific gene-disruption mutations** in different historical backgrounds. These are **test perturbations**, not newly spontaneously fixed adaptive changes in each genotype. Competition/reference measures and mixed effects controlled variation within assays.
- Comparing the distribution of fitness effects (DFE) across backgrounds revealed **mean fitness effects becoming more deleterious in one environment** but not significantly changing in the other.
- Thus the *same* hypothetical next mutation can be differently harmful/neutral in genomes that have adapted elsewhere, and any claim that robustness always grows or always falls with fitness is **refuted by the two-environment contrast**.

## Important published negative result

Evolutionary change in the local mutation-effect distribution was **environment dependent**. Authors found a decline in mutational robustness in one regime and no comparable mean decline in the second, despite both sets of populations accumulating adaptive changes.

A model in which every lineage steadily unlocks more functional paths is therefore too simple: historical adaptation may close, weaken or leave unchanged the usefulness of possible mutation routes.

The study did not identify a new higher-level reproductive unit or an organism-origin event. Nor does it show that yeast actively choose new mutation rates based on a concept of future novelty.

## Correct inference for digital life research

The observation supports testing **changes in local reachability and consequences of heritable variation**, rather than merely counting total possible instructions or measuring novelty in successful descendants.

The future assay to discriminate EERC variants must record:
- background-specific fitness of the **same** controlled mutation/control perturbation;
- sampling and founder ancestry, independent worlds, all extinct strains;
- whether a route becomes accessible *or* previously useful alternatives disappear;
- differences between current fitness, mutation target frequency and expected adaptive benefit;
- actual ecological opportunity and independent offspring survival when a route becomes useful;
- costs of physical maintenance and external author-supplied growth constraints.
