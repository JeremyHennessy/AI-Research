## Scientific consequence for Ora: persistence of knowledge is not truth

The 1996 robust-knowledge paper distinguishes ordinary predictive accuracy from the consistency of a rule across whole database states. The authors report estimating the chance of invalidating edits and pruning a supplied candidate rule. In one sample (Table 7), the original rule R5 has estimated robustness 0.9784990 with six antecedents; r7 has 0.9896200 with four, and r10 has 0.9814620 with three. The shortest rule is not the highest-scoring one. These are estimates under the chosen database assumptions, not observations of future performance; selected results text only, table not visually checked. The work uses a defined relational schema, rule language and estimation procedure.

The SIMS final report (indexed text, chapter on source descriptions) explicitly requires a human-created domain model and supplied mappings. It then derives source-combination rules algorithmically. The 1996 model-learning paper identifies automation of that supplied semantic layer as unfinished. DataGuides provide a contrasting capability: generating structural summaries directly from data within a supplied graph formalism. None demonstrates autonomous semantic grounding.

Prior-art crosswalk: (a) acquiring and pruning rules under changing observations, (b) obtaining structural representations without a hand-written fixed schema, and (c) trading planning effort against plan quality are documented research directions predating Ora. They differ from developing a new learning mechanism, inheriting a capability, or producing an objectively viable lineage. Persistence of an internal rule under a narrow environment is not proof of truth or transfer under a changed environment; estimated robustness is not measured ecological viability. No novelty claim or change to Ora's experimental plan follows.
