## GILA: evidence boundary and methodological relevance

Selected sections 2.3 and 6.1–6.4 report a heterogeneous set of learned specialist components coordinated by a designed executive. Learning consumes machine-readable demonstration traces, supplied domain knowledge, a defined goal, and practice problems. The authors compare performance with 33 novice participants across six combinations drawn from three test scenarios in one task domain. Table III reports mean task-quality scores of 95.4/100 for GILA and 90.5/100 for the human novices. The scoring expert was independent of the developers and grading was blinded, according to the paper. This is not a comparison with expert humans, arbitrary-domain intelligence, or independently reproduced general learning.

The no-demonstration comparison (section 6.4.1) is particularly informative: authors report about tenfold slower problem solving without learning but an apparently nonsignificant difference in solution-quality score. Hence the demonstration contributed primarily to efficiency in that comparison; existing infrastructure could already solve at least some of the problems. The biased-trace study further shows dependence of some learner components on demonstrated choices, while another designed search component could propose alternatives. The single-component comparison retains shared support components; it is not a completely isolated architecture comparison. The authors also note common-sense conventions absent from GILA. Raw observations and statistical calculations have not been independently audited, so retain E1 with a targeted full-method audit pending. Do not use the reported significance to extrapolate beyond the limited scenario structure.

A related [2012 safety-constraint learning paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8640.2012.00416.x) reports a component of GILA tested on two benchmark tasks. Abstract only; exact award not established. This is a programme link, not evidence of an unrestricted safety guarantee.

## Implications for Ora

Separate evidence of an acquired behavior from evidence of increased efficiency using acquired information. An unchanged end score does not necessarily mean that learning had no effect; conversely, faster completion does not establish a new underlying capability. Record initial knowledge, supplied objectives and externally authored procedures, and distinguish the contribution of learning from existing search or control.
