## Ora crosswalk

The 2008 spatial-transfer study (252) explicitly describes researcher adaptation of a procedural strategy across two related tasks. It reports correlation .92 between model and human response patterns, but average response times 3.64 s model versus 4.94 s human, and RMSD 1.45 s. These are reported human-model fit results, not resource costs or autonomous transfer. The model exploits distinct object-cluster sizes; it does not solve ambiguity when multiple candidate groups share the same distinguishing structure. Row 255 describes the earlier task with unique cluster sizes 1,2,3,4. This suggests a concrete representation aliasing boundary: if two states have identical encoded features but opposite correct actions, no deterministic policy on those features can succeed on both. For equally likely states, even a randomized policy has success at most 1/2, because the sum of probabilities assigned to the two distinct correct actions cannot exceed one. This elementary analytical bound is not a historical model reproduction or an Ora run. Distinguish environmental uniqueness supplied by task design, learned representation, and robustness to held-out aliases; compare costs and reproductive outcomes before claiming usefulness.

Row 256 separates planning from action and reports changing strategy preferences through practice, whereas row 258 describes supplied strategies. Do not confuse within-lifetime strategy selection with heritable generation of a new strategy. Row 251 is particularly relevant to later abstraction research: a demonstration of rediscovering an existing arithmetic property using an authored mapping procedure is not evidence of unrestricted abstraction. Prioritize full review of its mapping inputs and conclusion provenance. Row 260 introduces externally specified fatigue dynamics; distinguish physical resource depletion, an authored internal impairment function and independently evolved regulation.
