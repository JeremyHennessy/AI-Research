## Original analysis: fewer unwanted actions need not establish control
Let an environment have H unwanted default opportunities and B beneficial default opportunities, with H+B>0. A selective controller blocks fraction s of H and fraction f of B, both in [0,1]. Residual unwanted actions = H(1-s), preserved beneficial actions = B(1-f). For example H=20, B=80: a blanket blocker (s=f=1) produces zero unwanted actions and zero beneficial actions. A selective blocker (s=0.9, f=0.05) produces two unwanted actions and preserves 76 beneficial actions. Measuring only unwanted actions wrongly ranks blanket inhibition first. If avoiding an unwanted action yields L>0, preserving a beneficial action yields R>0, and cost of control is C>=0, gain over unregulated baseline is H*s*L - B*f*R - C. Positive gain requires H*s*L > B*f*R+C. This toy model assumes additive values, fixed opportunity counts and no alternative response reward; it is not a historical or Ora experiment.

A second identifiability problem: observed absence of an action is consistent with absence of stimulus, a blocked sensor, occupied effector, a selected alternative, resource exhaustion, or a learned inhibitory rule. None is identified by the endpoint alone. Evidence for selective acquired control would require a matched opportunity ledger, retained useful responses, provenance of the control rule and its acquisition history, and causal comparison against a suitable no-control or nonselective control. Cost and timing must be accounted separately; in an evolutionary system inherited policy changes must also be distinguished from within-lifetime acquisition. These are evaluation principles only, not a request to implement or execute anything. The paper's admission that fast behavior can be beneficial motivates the comparison; equations and examples are original analytical illustrations.
