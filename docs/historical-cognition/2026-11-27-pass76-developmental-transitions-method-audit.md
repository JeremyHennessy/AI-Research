## Findings
Simulation I (sections 3.1–3.3, Fig.4): designer supplies numerical weight and distance observations, a difference-search strategy, ACT-R retrieval/composition and utility learning, staged availability of arithmetic concepts and increasing processing capacity. Rule composition builds new task-specific productions; it does not invent arithmetic or the rule-learning machinery. Feedback is used to update utility. Fig.4 is a typical run rather than an ensemble uncertainty estimate. Initial preference for weight is assumed (note 10); its origin is not explained.
Table 2 (p.242) is theoretical accuracy over 625 configurations, not observed human scores or a held-out generalization trial. Rule II achieves 513/625 (82.08%), Rule III about 491.6/625 (78.66%, with guessing), addition heuristic 573/625 (91.68%), multiplication rule 625/625. More elaborate reasoning need not immediately improve accuracy. Addition can block progression to multiplication even when multiplication is available; the reported transition needs a selected set of problems on which addition fails. These fractions depend on the problem distribution.
Simulation II (section 3.4, Fig.6): graded distance differences are supplied, retrieval activation is augmented by a designer-defined saliency term, and noise is disabled. Increasing then decreasing saliency can produce reversible, hysteretic or retained shifts to using distance information. Correctness feedback is absent during this sequence, but memory activation still changes and prior feedback-driven utility learning is necessary to prepare the transition (section 4.1.3, p.252). The absence of feedback in one interval is not evidence of lifelong feedback independence. The article does not establish a corresponding no-feedback transition across all later phases or independently acquired capacity. Reaction-time and instruction-sensitivity predictions (pp.253–254) are predictions, not validated results in this paper.

## Original Ora analytical crosswalk (research only)
A toy fixed decision function y=1[x+b>=1] with constant b=0 and input x moving from 0 to 2 and back produces an apparently sudden capability gain and loss despite zero learning. With a retained b=0.4 after the first threshold crossing, the downward threshold becomes 0.6 instead of 1, yielding hysteresis without any newly invented operator. These are mathematical examples, not a replication of ACT-R or Ora. Separately, define a 100-item environment: strategy A solves 82 items, B solves 79, and C solves 100 but is reachable only through B. An immediate-reward-only policy rejecting B can prevent eventual access to C even though B is an intermediate step. This is a possible local-optimum mechanism, not a claim that the historical model or Ora must follow this trajectory. For future separately authorized evaluation, distinguish stable improvement on matched inputs from stimulus-induced threshold crossing; trace prior feedback, supplied feature definitions and external capacity changes; retain temporary performance declines and their costs instead of automatically interpreting them as regression or novelty. Compare matched state/inputs with and without prior experience; require persistent functional transfer and attributable acquired changes. No claims about present Ora capabilities are promoted.
