## Contrary evidence to familiarity-only accounts
Pass 85 identified Servan-Schreiber & Anderson (1990), DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.16.4.592, a model of acquired chunks using supplied learning rules. Knowlton & Squire cite that work and independently vary grammatical validity and frequency of familiar fragments. In Experiment 1, 18 controls and 11 amnesic participants classified new strings after exposure to exemplars. The authors report effects of both variables and an interaction: familiarity was particularly influential for invalid strings. This is human evidence, not replication of the earlier computational model. In Experiment 3, nine amnesic participants and 14 controls completed four sessions involving four grammars and identical versus changed alphabets. Reported transfer above chance was accompanied by a same-alphabet advantage. Sessions and participants are not independent replications; prior participation and inter-session intervals differ between groups, as the authors discuss. These results do not uniquely identify a learned formal grammar: analogy and token-independent repetition structure remain plausible explanations.

## Independent arithmetic and interpretation
From Table 3, controls endorsed 54.9% of invalid strings with familiar chunks, versus 28.1% with less familiar chunks: +26.8 percentage points in false acceptance. Corresponding patient contrast 56.8 minus 30.7 = 26.1 points. These are conditional descriptive contrasts, not paired-effect confidence intervals. Experiment 3 controls scored 59.9% versus 56.1% (same versus changed letters, grammars C/D), and 65.5% versus 60.7% (A/B): transfer penalties of 3.8 and 4.8 points. The first pair is from indexed p.177, not visually authenticated. These are author-reported means, not new trials or independently reproduced outcomes.

## Ora crosswalk: distinguish token identity from relational invariance
Hypothetical rule: accept length-four strings exactly when positions 1=3 and 2=4, with the first two different. Training ABAB and CDCD; evaluation EFEF (valid, unfamiliar symbols) versus EFFE (invalid, same symbol counts). A histogram-only classifier must give identical outputs for the latter pair, so on a balanced pair it cannot exceed 50% accuracy. A supplied equality-pattern detector succeeds at both without any learning, so success on the pair also cannot prove the organism learned its detector. This adds a distinct alternative to Pass 85's familiar-chunk counterexample: distinguish token-specific familiarity, relational invariance, acquired rule, and provenance of the mechanism.
